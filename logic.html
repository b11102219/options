A Field Guide to Logical Fallacies

Sixteen commonly sighted specimens, grouped into four families by how they go wrong — so you can name what you're hearing before you argue with it.

Relevance — points away from the argument
Presumption — assumes what it needs to prove
Ambiguity — hides in unclear language
Weak Induction — evidence too thin for the claim

How a specimen gets sorted

Ask the questions in order and stop at the first "yes." This gives every fallacy exactly one home — the four families are built to be exhaustive of the ways reasoning can fail informally, so nothing falls through the cracks or fits two boxes at once.

1. Unclear language?Does a word, phrase, or grammar shift meaning or scope mid-argument? → Ambiguity
2. Unproven premise?Otherwise: does it assume, as already true, the very thing it needs to prove? → Presumption
3. Irrelevant content?Otherwise: does it lean on something with no logical bearing on the claim — character, emotion, status, or headcount? → Relevance
4. Thin evidence?Otherwise: is the content actually relevant, but too weak, small, or shaky to support the conclusion's strength? → Weak Induction

Relevance Fallacies

Leans on something with no logical bearing on the claim — character, feeling, status, or head count stand in for evidence.

Ad Hominemdebates, comments

Attacks the person instead of the argument.

"Don't trust his data on emissions — he drives a pickup truck."

Red Herringmeetings, politics

Drags in an unrelated topic to change the subject entirely.

"Why worry about my late reports — the whole industry is struggling."

Straw Manop-eds, debates

Rebuts an exaggerated version nobody actually argued.

"She wants protected bike lanes? So she wants to ban cars entirely."

Appeal to Authorityads, health claims

Leans on someone's status, not relevant expertise or evidence.

"This supplement works — a famous athlete swears by it."

Appeal to Emotioncampaigns, fundraising

Substitutes feeling for evidence — the emotion itself proves nothing.

"Think of the children — how could you vote against this?"

Bandwagonmarketing, trends

Treats headcount as proof — how many believe it has no bearing on whether it's true.

"Millions of people bought it, so it has to be worth it."

Presumption Fallacies

Treats an unproven claim as already settled, then builds on it.

Begging the Questionpolicy talk

The conclusion is smuggled into the premise.

"Free speech matters because people should be free to speak their minds."

False Dilemmabudgets, ultimatums

Offers only two options when more exist.

"Either we cancel the program, or the whole budget collapses."

Loaded Questioninterviews, HR

Bakes an unproven accusation into the question itself.

"Have you stopped padding your hours?"

Ambiguity Fallacies

Unclear or shifting wording lets the argument seem to work when it doesn't.

Equivocationwordplay, ads

A key word quietly switches meaning mid-argument.

"A feather is light. Nothing light can be dark. So a feather can't be dark."

Amphibolyheadlines, contracts

Sentence structure allows two different readings.

"I photographed the hiker with the drone." (Who has the drone?)

Compositionteam reviews

Assumes what's true of the parts must be true of the whole.

"Every player is excellent, so this must be an excellent team."

Divisionstereotyping

Assumes what's true of the whole applies to every part.

"It's a wealthy neighborhood, so every resident must be rich."

Weak Induction Fallacies

The content is genuinely relevant — it's just too thin, small, or shaky to earn the conclusion drawn from it.

Hasty Generalizationsmall talk, reviews

A sweeping claim from a tiny, unrepresentative sample.

"Both people I met from that city were rude — people there are rude."

Post Hocsuperstition, sports

Assumes that because B followed A, A caused B.

"I wore my lucky socks and we won — the socks worked."

Slippery Sloperulemaking, panic

Predicts an extreme end from one step, without evidence for the chain in between.

"Allow one exception and the entire policy falls apart."

Field notes for teaching this

  • Start with the family question first — relevance, presumption, ambiguity, or evidence? — before hunting for the exact name. Naming the family builds the instinct faster than memorizing sixteen labels.
  • Use real specimens. Pull one line from an ad, a comment section, or a recent news story and ask learners to sort it into a family before naming it precisely.
  • Warn against fallacy-hunting as a sport: pointing out a fallacy doesn't automatically make the underlying conclusion false. A bad argument for a true thing is still a bad argument — and still worth flagging.
  • Pair each fallacy with its "steelman" fix: what would a non-fallacious version of this same point look like?

Spot it in the wild

"You only oppose the merger because you got passed over for VP."

Ad Hominem — attacks the critic's motive instead of the merits of the merger.

"We can't fund the library and the clinic both — pick one."

False Dilemma — presents two options when partial or phased funding may exist.

"Our top three customers loved it, so the whole market will."

Hasty Generalization — three customers is a thin sample for a market-wide claim.

"He's a Nobel physicist, so his opinion on nutrition must be right."

Appeal to Authority — the expertise doesn't match the subject of the claim.

The four-question test is exhaustive by construction — any informal fallacy sorts into exactly one family. These sixteen specimens are a curated sample within it, not the full population of named fallacies (dozens more exist, and new ones get named). Real arguments often stack more than one at once.
